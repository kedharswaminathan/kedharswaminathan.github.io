Customer Churn and Retention Cost Model

Which customers are about to leave, and when is it worth paying to keep them?

How I approached it

The IBM Telco dataset has 7,043 customers and 21 variables, and 26.5% of customers leave. Predicting that nobody leaves already scores 73.4% accuracy, so I judged models on ROC-AUC, which measures how well a model ranks risky customers above safe ones.

I compared logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting, and chose logistic regression (0.835) over gradient boosting (0.841). The gap is within noise on 1,407 test customers, and logistic regression shows which traits drive risk, which matters when the output decides who gets a discount. Class weighting raised recall from 57% to 79% without changing how well the model ranks customers.

What I found

Models flag a customer at 50% predicted risk by default, but nobody actually chooses that number. I modeled the real decision: $50 per retention offer, offers that work 30% of the time, and a lost customer worth $500. The cheapest cutoff is 0.40, which means contacting 440 customers. Then I varied what a lost customer is worth. At $250 the cheapest cutoff is 0.69 (103 customers). At $1,000 it's 0.14 (817 customers). The campaign size swings eightfold on a number the dataset doesn't contain, so the honest recommendation is to get real customer-value figures from finance before picking a cutoff.

What this doesn't show

  • All results come from one train/test split with default settings and no cross-validation.
  • The dollar values are assumptions, which is exactly why the analysis tests a range instead of reporting one answer.
  • Contract type, tenure, and monthly charges are associated with leaving. The model doesn't show that they cause it.
